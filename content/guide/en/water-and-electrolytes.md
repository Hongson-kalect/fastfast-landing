Staying hydrated is the simplest thing you can do to make fasting easier. A surprising number of "fasting side effects" – headaches, tiredness, dizziness – are really signs of too little water or salt.

## Why you need more water when fasting

A good part of your daily water normally comes from food: fruit, vegetables, soups, rice and pasta all hold water. When you skip meals, that water is missing too. On top of that, when insulin falls during a fast, the kidneys excrete more sodium – and water follows sodium.

## How much to drink

There is no magic number. A practical guide:

- Drink when you are thirsty, and a glass or two more than usual during the fast.
- Aim for **pale yellow urine**. Dark yellow means drink more; completely clear all day may mean more than you need.
- Drink more in hot weather, when exercising or if you sweat a lot.
- Spread it out – large amounts all at once are less useful.

Water, sparkling water, mineral water, black coffee and plain tea all count. Coffee and tea have a mild diuretic effect but still contribute overall.

## Electrolytes on longer fasts

For a 16-hour fast, normal meals in your window usually cover your needs. From about 24 hours on, electrolytes start to matter:

| Electrolyte | Role | Signs of shortage | Simple sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium | Fluid balance, blood pressure | Headache, dizziness on standing, fatigue | A pinch of salt in water, salted broth (in your window) |
| Potassium | Heart rhythm, muscles | Weakness, cramps, palpitations | Vegetables, avocado, potatoes, beans (in your window); some mineral waters |
| Magnesium | Muscles, nerves, sleep | Cramps, poor sleep, irritability | Nuts, seeds, leafy greens (in your window); mineral waters; supplements |

**Practical options during a fast:**
- A small pinch of salt (about ¼ teaspoon) dissolved in a large glass of water, once or twice a day on long fasts.
- Mineral water with naturally higher sodium and magnesium.
- Sugar-free electrolyte powders or tablets – check the label for sugar and sweeteners.

> Avoid large doses of potassium supplements unless your doctor recommends them; too much potassium can be dangerous.

## Who should be careful

Talk to a doctor before adding salt or electrolytes if you:

- Have kidney disease or heart failure
- Take blood pressure medication, diuretics or other drugs that affect fluids and electrolytes
- Have been told to limit salt

## Signs you need more fluids or salt

- Headache that builds through the fast
- Feeling dizzy or seeing spots when you stand up
- Unusual tiredness or muscle cramps
- Dark urine and a dry mouth

If symptoms do not improve after drinking water with a little salt, end the fast. If you have palpitations, fainting or confusion, seek medical help.
