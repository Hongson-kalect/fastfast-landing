🌱 What is intermittent fasting?

A plain-language introduction to intermittent fasting – how it works, why people do it and what to expect in the first weeks.

3 min read · Updated 2026-10-29

Intermittent fasting (IF) is not a diet that tells you what to eat. It is a pattern that decides when you eat: you alternate between a period of eating and a period of not eating. The most popular version, 16:8, means fasting for 16 hours and eating within an 8-hour window – for example, from noon to 8 pm.

How it works

Every time you eat, your body spends a few hours digesting and storing energy. Insulin rises, and your cells mainly run on glucose from the meal. When you stop eating for long enough, insulin falls, the liver uses up its glycogen store, and your body increasingly switches to burning fat and producing ketones. Researchers call this the metabolic switch, and it usually happens somewhere between 12 and 36 hours without food, depending on your last meal and activity.

Fasting does not burn fat by magic. In practice, a shorter eating window helps many people eat less without counting calories, and the regular rhythm makes healthy habits easier to keep.

Why people try it

  • Weight management: in clinical trials, IF works about as well as ordinary calorie reduction – and some people simply find it easier to stick to.
  • Metabolic health: several trials show better insulin sensitivity and blood pressure, especially when eating earlier in the day.
  • Simplicity: fewer meals to plan, fewer decisions, no special food.
  • Structure: a clear start and end time helps stop late-night snacking.

Claims about longer life, autophagy and "cell renewal" are interesting but come mostly from animal research. Read what research really says for an honest overview.

What to expect in the first weeks

The first few days are usually the hardest. Hunger tends to come in waves around your usual meal times and then passes. Many people notice mild headaches, irritability or low energy at first – often linked to drinking too little water or missing salt. After one to two weeks, most people adapt and hunger becomes easier to manage.

Fasting is not for everyone. If you are pregnant, under 18, have diabetes, a history of eating disorders or take regular medication, read who should not fast and talk to a doctor first.

How to start

  1. Pick an easy schedule – 12 or 14 hours is a perfectly good first step.
  2. Let most of the fast happen while you sleep: finish dinner, then skip late snacks.
  3. Drink water, black coffee or plain tea during the fast.
  4. Eat normal, balanced meals in your window – protein, vegetables, whole foods.
  5. Add an hour every few days until 16 hours feels comfortable.
