⏱️ What happens in your body during a fast, hour by hour

From digesting your last meal to the metabolic switch, ketosis and growth hormone – a realistic timeline of fasting and what the research supports.

3 min read · Updated 2026-10-29

  2. 4–12 hours: running on glycogen
  3. 12–18 hours: the metabolic switch
  4. 18–24 hours: deeper ketosis
  5. Growth hormone
  6. 24–72 hours: longer fasts

Fasting is not one state but a sequence of changes. The timeline below is an average for a healthy adult after a normal mixed meal. Your own timing can shift by several hours depending on what you last ate, how active you are, how well you slept and your metabolism.

0–4 hours: the fed state

Your body digests the last meal. Blood glucose and insulin rise, and insulin tells cells to take up glucose and store the extra – as glycogen in the liver and muscles, and as fat. Fat burning is low because insulin suppresses it.

4–12 hours: running on glycogen

Insulin falls back toward baseline. To keep blood sugar steady, the liver releases glucose from its glycogen store. Fat breakdown (lipolysis) slowly increases. For most people, this is simply the overnight fast.

12–18 hours: the metabolic switch

The liver holds roughly 100 g of glycogen, and by now it is running low. The body shifts toward fat as its main fuel: fat cells release fatty acids, and the liver turns some of them into ketone bodies (mainly beta-hydroxybutyrate). This change from glucose-based to fat-and-ketone-based energy is what researchers call the metabolic switch. It typically happens between 12 and 36 hours of fasting.

In FastFast, this is when the Fat burning and Ketosis phases start to fill.

18–24 hours: deeper ketosis

Ketone levels keep rising. Ketones are an efficient fuel for the heart, muscles and brain, and they also act as signalling molecules. Many people report steadier energy and less hunger at this stage, though the first few times can feel harder.

Growth hormone

Fasting increases growth hormone secretion. In a classic study, a two-day fast raised 24-hour growth hormone output about fivefold. Growth hormone helps preserve muscle protein and mobilise fat during fasting. The Growth hormone phase on the FastFast timer reflects this rise.

24–72 hours: longer fasts

  • Ketones continue to climb and the brain gets a growing share of its energy from them.
  • The body makes the glucose it still needs from glycerol, lactate and some amino acids (gluconeogenesis).
  • Animal studies link this window to increased autophagy and to renewal of immune cells after refeeding. In humans, these effects are much less certain – see autophagy and immune renewal.

What this means in practice

  • Most of the well-documented benefits come from regularly reaching the metabolic switch, which a 16–18 hour fast already approaches.
  • Longer is not automatically better. Fasts beyond 24 hours bring more side effects and risks – read who should not fast and water & electrolytes first.
